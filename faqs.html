The NCD Crisis: The Emergency We Are Solving

Non-communicable diseases account for 39% of deaths in Kenya and more than 50% of hospital admissions, yet the specialist infrastructure needed to treat these conditions remains critically limited.

The gaps in the system

Understanding the crisis

Six structural gaps drive Kenya's NCD emergency. Here is what the data shows and why it matters.

Why do patients fall through the cracks in Kenya's health system?

Referral pathways are broken. Patients are frequently lost between primary facilities and tertiary hospitals, with no clarity about which specialist should see them, when they should be seen, or how referrals should be tracked once they leave a clinic.

What happens to patients after diagnosis or hospitalization?

There is little structured follow-up. After diagnosis or hospitalization, patients often fall out of the system entirely, a gap that drives readmission, clinical decline, poor outcomes, and preventable death.

Are non-communicable diseases well understood?

Not consistently. Hypertension, diabetes, stroke, mental illness, and neurological disease are often undiagnosed, normalized, or stigmatized, and mental and neurological disorders are projected to increase by 40% by 2030. The economic toll is already severe: NCDs cost Kenya an estimated KSh 300 billion (about $2.3 billion) a year, reduce affected families' income by 28.6%, and leave those families 30% more likely to fall into poverty than families affected by communicable disease.

Is there enough specialist capacity?

Far from it. Kenya has approximately 2.5 interventional neuro specialists for more than 55 million people, 48 cardiologists in total, and fewer than 10 interventional cardiologists. The country's two neuronavigation systems have both been non-functional for more than two years.

Why is research infrastructure a problem?

Kenya faces a shortage of Good Clinical Practice-ready trial sites, biobanks, interoperable data systems, African genomic baselines, and research-ready hospital infrastructure, gaps that also leave many rural counties without neurologists, cardiologists, or endocrinologists.

Why does this matter globally?

Because Africa is being left out of the research that could close this gap. The continent holds roughly 19% of the world's population and about 25% of the global disease burden, yet it hosted only about 1.1% of the 76,331 clinical trials initiated globally in 2023 (845 trials, 73% of them in South Africa). Kenya has hosted just 113 clinical trials across its entire history.
